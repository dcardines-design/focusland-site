Active recall: how to study by testing yourself

By Rosebud Studio, the makers of Focusland. Updated November 19, 2026.

You read the chapter twice, you highlighted the good parts, and it all felt familiar. Then the exam asks a question and your mind goes blank. Active recall is a simple fix for that gap: instead of putting information into your head again, you practice pulling it out. Here's what it is, what the research says, where the evidence is thinner, and how to start today.

What is active recall?

Active recall means bringing something back from memory without looking at it. You close the book and try to explain the idea. You read a question and answer it before you check. You cover the answer side of a flash card and say it out loud first.

Researchers usually call this retrieval practice, and the boost it gives to memory is called the testing effect. The word "testing" sounds stressful, but it doesn't have to mean grades. A quiz you give yourself on the couch counts.

The opposite is passive review: rereading, highlighting, or watching the same lecture again. Those feel productive because the material starts to look familiar. But looking familiar and being able to remember it on your own are two different things. Passive review is also where your mind tends to wander.

What the research says

In a well-known study, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke had students read short science passages. Some read each passage again. Others took a recall test, writing down everything they could remember. Five minutes later, the rereaders did a little better. But two days and one week later, the students who had tested themselves remembered more. In another part of the study, students who only reread felt more confident they would remember, even though they remembered less.1

A follow-up by Karpicke and Roediger looked at students learning foreign word pairs. Once a word had been learned, continuing to practice recalling it made a big difference a week later, while studying it again added very little. Students recalled more than twice as many words when they kept testing themselves. And again, the students' own predictions didn't show this. They expected about the same result either way.2

Karpicke and Janell Blunt then compared retrieval practice with concept mapping, a popular way to organize ideas on paper. Students who practiced recalling a science text did better on a test a week later, including on questions that asked them to draw new conclusions, not just repeat facts.3

These aren't one-off results. A review of many studies by Christopher Rowland found that, overall, practice tests beat restudying, with a moderate effect.4 A review of classroom research by Pooja Agarwal and colleagues found that most studies in real schools and colleges showed a clear benefit, across different subjects and ages.5 And a large review of study techniques by John Dunlosky and colleagues rated practice testing as one of only two techniques with "high utility," while rereading and highlighting were rated low.6

Where the evidence is thinner

It's worth being honest about the limits. Many of these studies used university students, short texts and word lists in a lab. Classroom studies are encouraging, but they vary a lot in how they were run. The research is strongest for remembering facts and ideas. It says less about skills like writing an essay or solving a brand new kind of problem, where practice doing the actual task matters too.

Active recall also feels harder than rereading. You'll get things wrong and notice gaps. That can feel like failing, which is a big reason people quietly go back to the highlighter. The struggle is part of how it works, but it's fine to start small. And if test worries or low mood make studying feel heavy most days, a study trick won't fix that on its own. Talking to someone you trust, or a doctor, can help.

How to use active recall

You don't need special software. Here are a few simple ways to swap rereading for remembering.

1. Close the book, then write what you remember

After reading a section, close it and write down everything you can recall on a blank page, in any order. Then open the book and check what you missed. This "blank page" method is close to what students did in the studies above.

2. Turn headings into questions

Before you read a section titled "Causes of the French Revolution," write "What caused the French Revolution?" at the top of your notes. Read, then cover the page and answer it. Your notes become a ready-made quiz for later.

3. Use flash cards the honest way

Say or write the answer before you flip the card. If you just flip and nod along, it turns back into rereading. Keep going until you've gotten each card right at least once, then come back to them another day. Shuffling cards from different chapters together, called interleaved practice, makes the quiz a little harder in a useful way.

4. Check your answers every time

Recall works best when you find out if you were right. Getting feedback helps you fix mistakes instead of practicing them. Mark wrong answers and put them back in the pile.

5. Explain it to someone (or no one)

Teach the idea out loud to a friend, a pet, or an empty chair, without your notes. Wherever you stumble is the part to review.

6. Space it out

Recall works even better when you come back to it after some time has passed. Quiz yourself tomorrow, then in a few days, then next week. Our guide to spaced repetition explains how to plan those gaps, and how to study for exams without cramming shows how it fits into a full exam plan.

7. Give recall its own time

A simple pattern: spend the first part of a study round reading or learning, and the last 10 minutes testing yourself on it. With the Pomodoro technique, that could be one full round of learning, then a short break, then a round of pure recall.

Common questions

What is active recall in simple words?

It's studying by remembering instead of rereading. You try to bring an answer back from memory first, then check it.

Is active recall better than rereading?

For remembering things days or weeks later, studies mostly say yes. Rereading can look better a few minutes after studying, but recall tends to win over longer stretches.

Is active recall the same as spaced repetition?

No, but they work well together. Active recall is how you study (testing yourself). Spaced repetition is when you study (spreading reviews out over days and weeks).

How long should an active recall session be?

There's no proven perfect length. Short, regular sessions are easier to keep up. Even 10 to 15 minutes of self-quizzing at the end of a study round is a good start.
