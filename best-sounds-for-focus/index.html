The best sounds for focus: music, white noise or silence?

By Rosebud Studio, the makers of Focusland. Updated November 2, 2026.

Some people need total quiet. Others can't think without a playlist or the hum of a café. The research doesn't crown one perfect focus sound, but it does give a few clear clues: words are the biggest troublemaker, loud and busy sounds make reading harder, and the right sound depends a lot on the task in front of you.

Why sound affects focus at all

Your ears never really switch off. Even when you're not trying to listen, your brain keeps checking what it hears, just in case something matters. Most of the time that's harmless. But when a sound carries meaning, like a voice or a song you know by heart, it can quietly compete for the same mental space you need for reading, writing or remembering.

That's why the question isn't really "music or no music." It's "how much does this sound pull on my attention, and how much attention does my task need?"

Words are the biggest distraction

One of the steadiest findings in this area is about speech. In a classic set of experiments, Pierre Salamé and Alan Baddeley asked people to remember lists of numbers they read on a screen while different sounds played. Speech they were told to ignore made their memory worse, even when it was in a language they didn't understand. Plain noise did far less harm.1 Researchers call this the irrelevant speech effect, and it has been found again and again since.

Song lyrics work in a similar way. In a study of reading comprehension, Nick Perham and Harriet Currie found that students scored lower while listening to music with lyrics, whether it was music they liked or music they didn't. Music without lyrics, and quiet, came out about the same.2

So if you're reading, writing or learning something new, words in your ears are the first thing to cut. Save your favorite singalong playlist for tidying your desk, doing the dishes or other tasks that don't need much thinking.

What about music without words?

Here the evidence gets mixed, and it's worth being honest about that. A large review by Juliane Kämpfe and colleagues pulled together results from many studies on background music. Overall, the effect on performance was close to zero. Music seemed to hurt reading and memory a little in some studies, and it tended to lift mood, but there was no strong, steady boost to focus.3

In plain words: calm instrumental music probably won't make you smarter, but it probably won't wreck your focus either. If it makes you more willing to sit down and stay sitting, that alone can be worth it. Slow, soft and familiar tends to be safer than fast, loud and exciting.

Café noise and the "just right" volume

Lots of people swear they work better in a coffee shop. There's some research behind that feeling, with a catch. Ravi Mehta, Rui Zhu and Amar Cheema played café-style background noise at different volumes while people did creative thinking tasks. A moderate level, around the hum of a busy café, helped creative thinking compared with a quiet room. A loud level made things worse.4

Two things to keep in mind. These were creative tasks, like coming up with ideas, not careful reading or memorizing. And the sweet spot was moderate. If you have to raise your voice to talk over it, it's too loud.

Nature sounds: birds, rain and crickets

Rain, birdsong and a soft wind are popular focus sounds, and there's a small but interesting study on them. Stephen Van Hedger and colleagues had people listen to recordings of nature sounds or city sounds, like traffic and car horns, then do a demanding memory and attention task. People did better after the nature sounds.5

This was one study with short recordings, so it's a hint, not a rule. Still, nature sounds have two things going for them: they don't contain words, and they're usually steady and gentle. That makes them a sensible first thing to try.

White noise, brown noise and ADHD

White noise and its deeper cousin, brown noise, are a big topic online, especially among people with ADHD. The research here is early. In one study, Göran Söderlund and colleagues found that loud white noise helped children who were inattentive remember more in a memory task, while it made children who were very attentive do a little worse.6

That's interesting, and it fits what many people with ADHD say: some background noise helps them settle. But it was one small study with children and one kind of task, so it doesn't prove white noise helps everyone with ADHD. It's not a treatment. If focus is a constant struggle for you, it's worth talking to a doctor. As a low-cost experiment, though, a few sessions with steady noise at a comfortable volume is easy to try.

Is silence best?

For tasks that need your full working memory, like studying for an exam, solving a hard problem or reading something dense, quiet is often as good as anything else and sometimes better. In the reading study above, quiet was among the best conditions.2 Silence isn't magic, but it can't distract you.

The catch is that real quiet is rare. Neighbors talk, roommates watch TV, coworkers take calls. Since speech is the worst kind of noise, covering it with something steady, like rain or a fan, can be better than hearing every word of someone else's conversation.

How to pick your focus sound

A simple way to choose, based on what the research suggests:

  • Reading, writing or memorizing: quiet, or steady sounds with no words, like rain or soft instrumental music.
  • Brainstorming or creative work: try a gentle café hum at a moderate volume.
  • Easy, repetitive tasks: anything you enjoy, lyrics included. Music can make boring tasks feel lighter.
  • Noisy place with people talking: cover voices with steady noise or nature sounds through headphones.
  • Keep the volume low. Louder isn't better, and it's kinder to your ears.
  • Pick one and stick with it. Hopping between songs and videos to find the perfect track is its own distraction.

Then test it. Try the same kind of task with two different sounds over a few days and notice which one gets more done. Your own results matter more than any study average. For more small habits like this, see how to focus when studying.

Pair your sound with a timer

A focus sound works best as a signal: when the rain starts, the work starts. Pairing it with a set block of time makes that signal even stronger. A 25-minute round with a short break after it, the idea behind the Pomodoro Technique, gives your sound a clear beginning and end, so it becomes part of your routine instead of something you fiddle with.

Common questions

Is it better to study with music or in silence?

For reading and memorizing, silence or sounds without words are usually the safer choice. Music with lyrics tends to hurt reading the most. For easy tasks, music you enjoy is fine.

Does lofi help you focus?

There isn't solid research on lofi itself. It's usually calm, slow and without words, which are the traits that tend to distract least, so it's a reasonable choice. Whether it helps you is best found out by trying it.

Is brown noise or white noise better for focus?

There's no good research comparing them for focus. Most of the studies used white noise. Brown noise sounds deeper and softer, so many people find it more pleasant. Pick the one you can listen to comfortably at a low volume.

Do noise-cancelling headphones help?

They can, mostly by turning down voices and sudden sounds around you, which are the most distracting kinds of noise. Even without music, they can make a busy place feel calmer.
