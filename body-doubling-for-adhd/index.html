Body doubling for ADHD: why it can help and how to try it

By Rosebud Studio, the makers of Focusland. Updated November 4, 2026.

You can stare at a task for an hour alone, then finish it in twenty minutes the moment a friend sits down nearby. Many people with ADHD know this feeling well. It has a name, body doubling, and while the research is still thin, it's cheap, gentle and easy to test for yourself.

What body doubling means for ADHD

Body doubling means doing a task with someone else present. They don't help, check your work or tell you what to do. They're just there, doing their own thing. If the idea is new to you, our beginner's guide to body doubling covers the basics.

The idea came from the ADHD community, not from a lab. In a 2024 survey of 220 people, researchers found it's used mostly by neurodivergent people, who described it roughly as using the presence of others to start, stay focused on, or finish a task.1 Many had never heard the term, but recognized it right away as something they'd been doing for years.1

It also covers more than desk work. People in that survey used it for studying and working, but also for dishes, cleaning and exercise. Their body double might be in the same room or far away, live or recorded, a friend or a stranger in a café.1

What the research says (and doesn't)

Here's the honest part: nobody has proven that body doubling works for ADHD. A 2025 preprint, which hasn't been through full peer review, says no peer-reviewed controlled trials have tested it in adults with ADHD, and that the few existing studies are inconclusive.2

That same paper ran a small test of its own. Twelve participants did a bricklaying task in virtual reality three ways: alone, with a human body double, and with an AI one. They finished faster with a body double and felt more accurate and more focused.2 That's encouraging, but twelve people doing one short task in a headset can't tell us much about your Tuesday afternoon. The authors themselves note they only looked at short-term effects.2

The same paper also points out that the presence of another person can bring social anxiety and fear of being judged, and that people's preferences differed.2 So if body doubling feels awful for you, nothing is wrong with you. It just may not be your tool, or it may need a different setup.

So treat body doubling as a low-risk experiment, not a proven method. It's also not a treatment for ADHD. Effective treatments do exist, including medication and therapy, and a health care provider is the right person to talk to about them.6

Why it might help

Nobody knows for sure why it helps the people it helps. These are reasonable ideas, not settled facts.

It gives you a way in

Starting is often the hardest part. In a small study of 54 students, more symptoms of inattention went along with more general procrastination, though the authors called the finding preliminary.3 People in the 2024 survey said they used body doubling to generate momentum and stay on task, which is exactly this problem.1

It makes waiting feel less empty

One theory of ADHD, called delay aversion, describes a motivational style of trying to escape or avoid waiting.4 Long, quiet, unrewarding work is a lot of waiting. Company may simply make that stretch feel less bare, though this link is our own guess and hasn't been tested.

It takes away a decision

"We start at 2" is a plan with a time and a place. Plans like that have some support: in one study, children with ADHD who used if-then plans got better at holding back an unwanted response on a lab task.5 That was a narrow task in children, so it's a hint, not proof. Still, a shared start time does a similar job to an if-then plan.

Ways to try it

  • Someone at home. Ask a roommate, partner or family member to sit nearby with their own task. No talking needed.
  • A quiet call. Start a video or audio call with a friend, say what you're each doing, then mute.
  • A public place. Libraries and cafés give you strangers to work beside, with no planning. If noise is a problem, our guide to the best sounds for focus can help.
  • Recorded videos. Long "study with me" videos are a recorded body double, and they're always on.
  • A focus app. Good for days when you can't reach anyone, or don't want to be seen.

How to make a session work

  1. Pick one small task. "Answer two emails" beats "sort out my inbox". Write it down where you can see it.
  2. Say it to someone. Tell your body double what you're starting, even if it's one line in a chat.
  3. Set a short timer. Twenty-five minutes is a good first try, as in the Pomodoro Technique. Go shorter on hard days.
  4. Agree on quiet. Decide up front that you won't chat until the timer ends, so company doesn't turn into a distraction.
  5. Keep your phone out of reach. Put it across the room if you aren't using it for the session.
  6. Take a real break. Stand up, stretch and get some water before the next round.
  7. Try it a few times. One bad session doesn't tell you much. Try different people, places and lengths, then keep what helps.

When it doesn't work

If you feel watched, try a setup with less eye contact: cameras off, audio only, a recorded video, or a café where nobody knows you. If your body double keeps pulling you into conversation, choose someone else or set a clear quiet rule. If you've come to rely on one person, add a second option so your work doesn't stop when they're busy.

And if the struggle to focus is causing real trouble at school, work or home, please talk to a doctor or other health care provider. A good tip can make a day easier, but it can't replace proper support.

Common questions

Does body doubling work for ADHD?

Many people with ADHD say it helps them, but there's no strong proof yet. The research so far is small and mixed, so the best way to find out is to try it a few times.

Can a video or an app be a body double?

People do use recorded videos and online sessions as body doubles.1 Whether they work as well as a person in the room hasn't been settled, so see which one feels best for you.

Do I need an ADHD diagnosis to try it?

No. Anyone can try it. If you think you might have ADHD, a health care provider can do a proper evaluation, since stress, poor sleep, anxiety and other conditions can cause similar symptoms.6

Can body doubling replace ADHD treatment?

No. It's a daily habit, not a treatment. Keep following any plan you have with your doctor, and ask them before changing it.
