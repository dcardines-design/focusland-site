How long should study breaks be? What the research says

By Rosebud Studio, the makers of Focusland. Updated October 26, 2026.

A good study break is short enough that you come back, and long enough that you actually feel better. For most people that means about 5 minutes after every 25 to 50 minutes of work, and a longer 15 to 30 minute break every hour and a half or so. Here's what the research says, and how to make your breaks do their job.

Why breaks help at all

Focus isn't a tank you fill once in the morning. As you work, you get tired, your mind wanders more, and the task starts to feel heavier. A break gives you a chance to reset before that drift turns into staring at the same page for ten minutes.

One well-known lab study asked people to watch for a rare signal on a screen for 50 minutes, a boring task where attention usually slips over time. People who briefly switched to a different small task twice during the hour kept their performance steady, while the group that never paused got worse.1 The researchers' take: brief mental breaks from a goal help you come back to it fresh.

There's real-world evidence too. A study of test scores from Danish public schools found that scores slipped a little for every hour later in the day a test was taken, as students got more tired. But a 20 to 30 minute break before the test more than made up for it on average.2

Short breaks: about 5 minutes

A large review pulled together 22 groups of people from studies on micro-breaks, which it defined as breaks of up to 10 minutes. These short breaks gave people a small but real boost in energy and a small drop in tiredness.3

The honest part: the same review found that short breaks did not clearly improve performance overall. They helped on simpler, routine tasks, but not clearly on heavy thinking tasks. It also found that longer breaks were linked to bigger gains in performance.3 So a 5 minute break is great for keeping your energy up, but it isn't magic for hard problems on its own.

That's why many study plans pair short breaks with a longer one every few rounds.

Long breaks: 15 to 30 minutes

After a few focus rounds, take a proper rest. In the Pomodoro Technique, that means a 15 to 30 minute break after four rounds. Use this one for real recovery: eat something, walk outside, stretch, or talk to someone.

If you can, go outside. In one experiment, people who took a walk in a park did better on an attention test afterward than people who walked through busy city streets.4 That walk was close to an hour long, so don't expect the same from a minute by the window, but green spaces seem to be a gentle place for tired attention to rest.

Fixed breaks or whenever you feel like it?

You might think the best plan is to stop whenever you feel tired. One study tested exactly that with 87 university students during a real day of self-study. Some took breaks whenever they chose. Others took fixed breaks: 6 minutes after every 24 minutes of study, or 3 minutes after every 12.5

The students who chose their own breaks studied in longer stretches and took longer breaks. They also reported more tiredness and distraction, and less concentration and motivation, than students on a fixed schedule. The amount of work they finished was about the same.5 It was one day and a small group, so it's not the final word.

A 2025 study of 94 students found something more mixed. Students on fixed 25/5 Pomodoro breaks got tired a little faster than students who chose their own breaks, but by the end of the session there was no real difference in tiredness, motivation or how much they got done.7 So a timer helps many people, especially if you forget to rest or never start, but if you already pace yourself well, flexible breaks can work just as well.

What to do on your break

How you spend the break matters as much as how long it is. A study of office workers found that relaxing (like stretching or daydreaming) and chatting with others helped people end the workday in a better mood. Doing more brain work during breaks, like reading or checking messages, made things worse.6 That was about work, not school, but the idea carries over.

Good break ideas:

  • Stand up and stretch, or walk around the room.
  • Refill your water or make a cup of tea.
  • Look out a window, or step outside for a minute.
  • Say hi to someone, or send a quick message to a friend.
  • Close your eyes and take a few slow breaths.

Breaks that tend to backfire: scrolling social media, opening a new video, or starting a "quick" game. They're more brain work, not less, and they are hard to stop after 5 minutes.

A simple schedule to try

  • Focus 25 minutes, break 5. Good for starting, low energy days, or tasks you're avoiding.
  • Focus 50 minutes, break 10. Good when you're already in the flow, like writing or problem sets.
  • Every 3 or 4 rounds, take 15 to 30 minutes. Eat, move, go outside.

Set a timer for the break too, not just the work. A break with no end tends to grow. If you struggle to come back, writing down your very next step before the break makes it easier to restart. And if starting is the hard part, our guide on how to stop procrastinating has a few small tricks.

These numbers are starting points, not rules. Try a schedule for a few days and notice how you feel at the end of a session. If you're foggy before the timer rings, shorten your focus rounds. If breaks keep cutting you off mid-thought, lengthen them.

Common questions

How long should a study break be?

For most people, about 5 minutes after every 25 to 50 minutes of focus, plus a longer 15 to 30 minute break every 3 or 4 rounds. Adjust to how you feel.

Is it better to study for hours without breaks?

Usually not. Attention tends to slip the longer you go without a pause. Whether fixed or flexible breaks work best is still open, but some regular rest helps most people.

Is scrolling my phone a good break?

It's better than nothing for some people, but it's easy to lose 20 minutes, and it keeps your brain busy. Moving, stretching, or talking to someone tends to work better.

How many study breaks should I take in a day?

A short break after each focus round, and a longer one every couple of hours. If you study for four hours, that might be around six short breaks and one or two long ones.
