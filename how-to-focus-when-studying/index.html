How to focus when studying: 8 small habits backed by research

By Rosebud Studio, the makers of Focusland. Updated November 1, 2026.

You sit down to study, read the same paragraph three times, and somehow end up on your phone. That's not a character flaw. Attention drifts on its own, and the world around you is full of things built to pull it away. The trick isn't more willpower. It's setting things up so focus is the easy choice.

Why focusing is so hard

Your mind wanders much more than you think. In a large study that pinged people on their phones at random moments during the day, people said their minds were somewhere other than what they were doing almost half the time.1 So if you drift while studying, you're normal.

Switching also has a price. In lab experiments, people who switched back and forth between two tasks were slower than people who stuck with one, and the cost grew as the tasks got more complex.2 Every quick check of a message means your brain has to find its place again when you come back.

Interruptions can feel harmless because you still get things done. One study of office workers found that people who were interrupted actually finished a writing task a bit faster, but they reported more stress, frustration and time pressure doing it.3 Focus isn't only about speed. It's also about how the work feels.

1. Decide on one task before you sit down

"Study biology" is too big to hold in your head. "Read pages 40 to 52 and write five flashcards" is a task you can start. Write it down somewhere you can see it. When your mind wanders, you have one clear thing to come back to, instead of a fog of everything you should be doing.

2. Make an if-then plan

Decide when, where and how you'll start, in the form "When X, I will Y." For example: "When I get home from class, I'll sit at the kitchen table and open my notes." A review of 94 studies found that if-then plans like this gave a medium-to-large boost to actually following through, compared with only meaning to.4 You can make one for distractions too: "If I want to check my phone, I'll write the thought down and keep going."

3. Put your phone in another room

This is the most common advice out there, and the research is honestly mixed. A well-known 2017 study found that students whose phones were in another room did better on memory and reasoning tests than students whose phones sat face down on the desk.5 But a careful replication in 2022 tested the same idea and found no difference between phone locations.6

So the phone's mere presence may not drain your brain the way headlines said. What's clear is simpler: if the phone is out of reach, you can't pick it up on autopilot. Notifications break your flow, and every check is a switch. A drawer, a bag across the room, or Do Not Disturb all make the easy choice the focused one.

4. Use a timer with a finish line

"Study until it's done" can feel endless, which makes it easy to drift. A timer turns it into a short, clear round: 25 minutes of focus, then a break. That's the heart of the Pomodoro Technique. When the timer is running, you only have to stay with the task until it rings.

5. Keep a distraction notepad

Random thoughts will pop up: an email to send, a snack to buy, a question to google. Instead of acting on them, jot them on a scrap of paper and go straight back to your task. Deal with the list on your break. Your brain lets go more easily once the thought is safely written down.

6. Study next to someone

Working alongside another person, even silently, makes it easier to start and harder to drift. You don't need to talk or share notes. Their quiet presence is a gentle nudge to keep going. This is called body doubling, and it's why libraries, cafés and study-with-me videos feel so helpful to many people.

7. Take real breaks

Focus isn't something you can hold forever. Short breaks between rounds help you come back fresher, especially if you stand up, move, get some water or look out a window instead of scrolling. Here's more on how long study breaks should be.

8. Set up a study spot that says "work"

Keep only what this task needs in front of you: the book, the notebook, the one tab. Close everything else. Good light, a glass of water and a comfortable chair help too. If home is too noisy or tempting, a library or a quiet corner somewhere else can make focusing feel much easier.

Once these habits feel normal, you can try longer stretches of one hard task. That's deep work, and you can start small.

When focus feels impossible

If the hard part is sitting down at all, start with how to get motivated to study. Everyone struggles to focus sometimes, especially when tired, stressed or hungry. Sleep, food and movement affect attention more than any trick. If trouble focusing is constant, gets in the way of school, work or home life, or comes with feeling low or anxious most days, it's worth talking to a doctor. Things like ADHD, anxiety, depression or poor sleep can all play a part, and these tips aren't a treatment for any of them.

Common questions

How long can you focus when studying?

It depends on the person, the task and how rested you are. Many people do well with 25 to 50 minute rounds and short breaks in between. Start with 25 minutes and adjust from there.

Does music help you focus?

It varies. Some people focus better with calm music or soft background sounds, while others find lyrics distracting, especially when reading. Try both and notice which way you get more done. For what the research says, see the best sounds for focus.

Why can't I focus even when I want to?

Tiredness, stress, hunger, a vague task or a buzzing phone can all get in the way. Try a smaller first step and a short timer. If it keeps happening for weeks, talk to a doctor.

Is it better to study alone or with others?

For focus, many people find it easier to work next to others while each doing their own work. Group study with lots of chatting can be helpful for explaining ideas, but it's less useful for quiet focus.
