Parkinson's law: why work fills the time you give it

By Rosebud Studio, the makers of Focusland. Updated November 18, 2026.

You give yourself all afternoon to answer three emails, and somehow it takes all afternoon. You give yourself 25 minutes, and it takes 25 minutes. That pattern has a name: Parkinson's law. Here's where it comes from, what the research actually says, and how to use it kindly, without turning your day into a race.

What is Parkinson's law?

Parkinson's law is the idea that "work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion." It comes from a funny essay that the British historian C. Northcote Parkinson wrote for The Economist in 1955.1 His example was an older lady with a free day who manages to spend all of it writing and sending a single postcard to her niece.

The essay was mostly a joke about government offices that keep growing whether or not there's more work to do. But the opening line stuck, because almost everyone recognizes it from their own life. A task with no clear end tends to stretch. It picks up extra checking, extra polishing and extra wandering off.

Is Parkinson's law real?

Parkinson wasn't running experiments, but psychologists later tried to test his idea in the lab, and they found something like it.

In a 1966 study, psychologists Elliot Aronson and E. Gerard gave students either a short or a long time to prepare a small talk, then gave them a similar task with no time limit. The students who'd had more time on the first task took longer on the second one too, even though nobody was rushing them anymore. The authors called this the "excess time effect."2 In other words, a roomy deadline seemed to set a slower pace that carried over.

Decades later, Laura Brannon and colleagues ran a cleaner test. Students judged sets of photos. Some were told, partway through, that a later set had been cancelled. Those students slowed down on the set in front of them, compared with students who still had the full workload ahead. The effect showed up again when the researchers repeated the study.3

Edwin Locke and Judith Bryan offered a simple way to think about it: Parkinson's law is a goal-setting effect.4 The time you're given acts like a quiet goal for how hard to push. A loose goal invites a loose pace.

What the evidence doesn't show

It's worth being honest here. The studies above are small, mostly done with university students in labs, and some are more than 50 years old. Not every attempt to repeat the original findings has worked. They support the idea that extra time can slow people down on simple tasks, but they don't prove that work always fills every gap, or that tighter deadlines are always better.

There's also a pull in the other direction. People are famously bad at guessing how long things take. In a well-known set of studies, Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin and Michael Ross found that students regularly underestimated how long their own projects would take, even when they knew similar projects had run late before.5 This is called the planning fallacy. So if you squeeze every task too hard, you may just end up rushed, stressed and late. The goal is a deadline that's snug, not impossible.

How to use Parkinson's law (gently)

The useful part of Parkinson's law isn't "work faster." It's "give your work an edge." Here are some small ways to do that.

1. Give each task a time box

Instead of "work on the essay today," try "outline the essay in 50 minutes." A clear end point makes the task feel smaller and stops it from leaking into everything else. If you like planning your whole day this way, time blocking is the same idea at a bigger scale.

2. Make the goal specific

Goal-setting research has found, across many studies, that specific and challenging goals usually lead to better performance than a vague "just do your best."6 "Read chapter 4 and write five flashcards before the timer ends" gives your brain something to aim at. "Study biology" doesn't.

3. Use short rounds, not one long stretch

A whole free afternoon is the perfect setup for Parkinson's postcard. Splitting it into rounds of 25 or 50 minutes, with a short break between, gives you several small deadlines instead of one far away. That's the heart of the Pomodoro Technique.

4. Guess, then check your guess

Before you start, write down how long you think the task will take. When you finish, note how long it really took. After a week, you'll know whether you tend to give yourself too much time or too little. That fixes both problems at once: Parkinson's law on one side and the planning fallacy on the other.

5. Decide what "done" looks like

Work stretches most in the polishing stage. Before you begin, write one line about what finished means: "three paragraphs, one read-through, send." When you reach it, stop. Good enough and sent beats perfect and still open.

6. Leave real slack for the hard stuff

Tight time boxes suit tasks you understand: emails, chores, review, first drafts. Deep, creative or brand-new work often needs more room to think. Use snug deadlines for the small things so you have more calm time left for the big ones.

7. Be kind when the timer wins

Some rounds won't finish the task. That's not failure, it's information. Write down where you stopped, take your break, and start the next round from there. If you notice you keep putting the task off altogether, our guide on how to stop procrastinating may help more than a tighter deadline.

Common questions

What is Parkinson's law in simple words?

The more time you give a task, the more time it tends to take. A task with a loose deadline picks up extra steps, extra polishing and extra breaks until it fills the time.

Who came up with Parkinson's law?

C. Northcote Parkinson, a British naval historian, in a humorous essay for The Economist in 1955. He later expanded it into a book.

Is Parkinson's law backed by science?

Partly. A few lab studies found that people given extra time worked more slowly, even on later tasks. The studies are small and old, though, so treat it as a useful pattern, not a law of nature.

Should I give myself less time for everything?

No. Snug deadlines help with simple, familiar tasks. People also tend to underestimate how long work takes, so for big or new tasks, give yourself a realistic window and break it into short rounds.
