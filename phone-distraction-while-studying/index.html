How to stop getting distracted by your phone while studying

By Rosebud Studio, the makers of Focusland. Updated November 17, 2026.

You sit down to study, and ten minutes later you're somehow watching a video about bread. Your phone is very good at pulling you away, and that isn't a lack of willpower on your part. The kind news is that you don't have to quit your phone. You just have to make it a little harder to reach while you work.

Why your phone is so hard to ignore

Every buzz or ping is a tiny question: who was that? Even if you don't pick the phone up, part of your mind goes off to wonder. In one experiment, researchers at Florida State University gave people a task that needed steady attention, then secretly sent texts or calls to their phones while they worked. People who got a notification made more mistakes, even though they never touched their phone. The drop was about as big as when people actually used their phones during the task.1

So the problem isn't only scrolling. It's the steady stream of little pulls on your attention, each one asking you to switch away from the page in front of you.

Does just having your phone nearby matter?

You may have heard that a phone sitting on your desk drains your brainpower even when it's silent and face down. That idea comes from a well-known 2017 study called "Brain Drain." People who left their phone in another room did better on memory and reasoning tasks than people who kept it on the desk.2

Here's the honest part: when other researchers repeated the main experiment carefully in 2022, they did not find the same effect. Where the phone sat made no difference to people's scores.3 So the science on a silent, nearby phone is mixed. What is clearer is that notifications and checking break your focus. Putting the phone away still helps, mostly because it stops the buzzing and makes checking take effort.

1. Put it out of reach, not just out of sight

The easiest checks are the ones you don't notice yourself doing. If the phone is in your hand's reach, you'll pick it up on autopilot. Put it in a bag, a drawer, another room, or a little basket across the room. The point is a few seconds of effort. That tiny pause is often enough to remind you that you're studying.

2. Silence the pings

Since notifications themselves pull your attention away, turn them off for your study time. Use Do Not Disturb or a Focus mode, and let only calls from a few important people through. If you study at the same time most days, set the Focus mode to switch on by itself so you don't have to remember.

3. Check in batches, not all the time

You don't have to go dark for the whole day. In a two-week field study, people who got their phone notifications in three batches a day felt more attentive and productive, less stressed and more in control than people who got them as usual. People who switched notifications off completely, though, felt more anxious and worried about missing out.4 A similar study found that people who checked email only three times a day felt less stressed than people who checked whenever they liked.5 So plan a few set times to catch up, like at each break, and let yourself relax in between.

4. Use timed study blocks

"Don't touch your phone all evening" is hard. "Don't touch it for 25 minutes" is easy. Study in short, timed blocks with a real break at the end, and make the break your phone time. Knowing you'll check soon makes it much easier to wait. That's the heart of the Pomodoro Technique.

5. Write down the urge, then go back

Often you reach for your phone because you suddenly remember something: a message to send, a thing to look up. Keep a scrap of paper next to you and jot it down instead. "Text Sam back." "Look up that song." Now your brain can let it go, and you can deal with it on your break.

6. Make an if-then plan for the pull

Decide ahead of time what you'll do when the urge to check shows up. For example: "If I want to check my phone, I'll take one deep breath and finish the sentence I'm on." A review of 94 studies found that if-then plans like this gave a medium-to-large boost to actually reaching goals, compared with just meaning to.6 It works because the decision is already made, so you don't have to win an argument with yourself every time.

7. Study next to someone

It's much harder to drift off into your phone when someone is working quietly beside you. A friend at the library, a sibling at the kitchen table or a study room online can all give you a gentle sense that you're in this together. This is called body doubling, and it pairs nicely with every tip above. For more ways to settle in, see how to focus when studying.

What if you need your phone to study?

Lots of people study with flashcard apps, notes or a timer on their phone. That's fine. Try keeping only the study app on screen, turn on a Focus mode that hides everything else, and move your most tempting apps off the home screen so they take a few extra taps to open. If you keep slipping, try a paper notebook or a laptop for part of your session.

And if you find you can't stop checking even when it's making you miserable, or it's getting in the way of sleep, school or work, it's worth talking to a doctor or counselor. You're not the only one, and help is out there.

Common questions

Should I put my phone in another room while studying?

It's a good idea, mostly because it stops notifications and makes checking take effort. Research on whether a silent phone nearby hurts focus on its own is mixed, but a phone you can't reach can't interrupt you.

Is it bad to check my phone on study breaks?

Not at all. Checking at set times, like on breaks, is a kind middle ground. Just set a timer so a 5 minute break doesn't turn into 40.

Is turning off all notifications the best option?

Not always. In one study, people who switched notifications off completely felt more anxious about missing out. Silencing them while you study and catching up in batches may feel better.

How long does it take to stop reaching for my phone?

It varies a lot from person to person. The reaching is a habit, so it fades with practice. Expect a few slips, and treat each one as a reminder to put the phone a bit farther away.
