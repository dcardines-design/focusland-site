The Pomodoro Technique: a cozy beginner's guide

By Rosebud Studio, the makers of Focusland. Updated October 24, 2026.

The Pomodoro Technique is a simple way to work: focus for 25 minutes, take a 5 minute break, and repeat. All you need is a timer and one task. Here's how it works, what the research actually says, and how to make it your own.

Where the name comes from

Francesco Cirillo created the technique in the 1980s as a university student who was struggling to focus. He challenged himself to study for a short stretch using a tomato-shaped kitchen timer, and "pomodoro" is Italian for tomato.1 Each focus block is called a pomodoro.

How to do it

  1. Pick one task. Write it down. "Read chapter 4" is better than "study".
  2. Set a timer for 25 minutes and work only on that task until it rings.
  3. Take a short break of about 5 minutes. Stand up, stretch, get water.
  4. Repeat. After four pomodoros, take a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes.2

If a thought pops up mid-pomodoro ("I need to reply to that email"), jot it on a piece of paper and get back to work. You can deal with it on your break.

Why it can help

It makes starting small

"Work on my essay" feels endless. "Work on my essay for 25 minutes" feels doable. A clear finish line makes it easier to begin.

Breaks are built in

Many of us either skip breaks or take breaks that turn into an hour of scrolling. A timer decides for you. A 2023 study of 87 university students found that students who took breaks on a fixed schedule felt less tired and more focused and motivated than students who chose their own breaks, and they got the same amount done in less time.3

Short breaks refresh you

A 2022 review of 22 studies found that short breaks of 10 minutes or less give a small but real boost in energy and lower tiredness. The boost to performance was smaller, and showed up mostly on lighter tasks. Longer breaks helped more after hard, draining work.4

What the research doesn't say

The Pomodoro Technique isn't magic, and the research is still small. A 2025 study of 94 students compared Pomodoro breaks, flexible "Flowtime" breaks and choosing your own breaks, and found no difference in how much students got done. Tiredness rose a bit faster with fixed Pomodoro breaks, although it ended up at about the same level by the end.5

So the honest summary: a fixed rhythm helps many people, especially if you tend to forget breaks or never start. If you naturally pace yourself well, flexible breaks may work just as well. Try it for a week and see how you feel.

Make it your own

  • Change the length. 25 minutes is a starting point, not a rule. If you're in a flow, try 50 minutes with a 10 minute break.
  • Make breaks real breaks. Step away from the screen. Checking email isn't a break.
  • Keep a tally. Counting pomodoros shows how much focus a task really takes, which helps you plan the next one.
  • Do it with others. Starting a timer at the same time as someone else adds a little gentle accountability. That's body doubling, and it pairs perfectly with Pomodoro.

Common questions

Why 25 minutes?

It's long enough to make real progress and short enough that starting doesn't feel scary. It's the length Cirillo's method uses, but you can adjust it.

What if I get interrupted?

If it's quick, note it down and keep going. If you really have to stop, end the pomodoro and start a fresh one when you're back.

Is the Pomodoro Technique good for ADHD?

Many people with ADHD find short, timed blocks helpful because they make starting easier. There isn't strong research on Pomodoro for ADHD specifically, so treat it as something to try, not a treatment.

Can I use it for things other than studying?

Yes. It works for writing, cleaning, emails, practicing an instrument, or anything you keep putting off.
