Spaced repetition: how to remember more without cramming

By Rosebud Studio, the makers of Focusland. Updated October 25, 2026.

Cramming feels like it works. You read the notes five times the night before, they feel familiar, and you walk into the test feeling ready. Then a week later it's mostly gone. Spaced repetition is the gentler, slower way: you study the same thing a few times, with gaps in between, so it sticks for much longer.

What spaced repetition is

Spaced repetition means coming back to the same material several times, spread out over days or weeks, instead of packing all your study into one long sitting. Psychologists call the benefit the spacing effect. The opposite, studying it all at once, is called massed practice. Cramming is massed practice the night before a test.

The idea is simple. Each time you return to something after a gap, you have to work a little to pull it back up. That small effort seems to be what helps the memory last.

What the research says

The spacing effect is one of the best tested ideas in learning research. A large review by Nicholas Cepeda and colleagues pulled together studies of people learning words and facts, and found that spreading study out beat massing it together again and again.1

A later study by the same group taught more than 1,350 people a set of facts, gave them one review after a gap of up to three and a half months, then tested them up to a year later. The best gap depended on how far away the test was. For a test one week away, the best gap was about 20 to 40% of that week, so roughly a day or two. For a test a year away, the best gap was about 5 to 10% of the year, so a few weeks.2 In plain words: the further away the test, the longer you can leave between reviews.

Spacing also works with flashcards. Nate Kornell had students learn vocabulary either in one big stack (more space between seeing each card) or in four small stacks (less space). The big stack won, and spacing also beat cramming on the last day before the test. Spacing helped for 90% of the students, yet after the first session 72% of them believed the massed way had worked better.3

That last part matters. Spacing often feels worse while you do it. In another study, people learned painting styles better when different artists' paintings were mixed and spaced, but still rated the bunched-up way as more effective, even after their own test scores showed the opposite.4 If spaced study feels a bit clumsy, that's normal. It doesn't mean it isn't working.

Pair it with testing yourself

Spacing works best when each review is a little quiz, not a reread. Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke had students read short passages and then either reread them or try to write down what they remembered. When the final test came five minutes later, rereading did a bit better. When it came two days or a week later, the students who had tested themselves remembered much more, even though the rereaders felt more confident.5

A big review of ten popular study methods by John Dunlosky and colleagues rated just two as high utility: spreading practice out over time and practice testing. Rereading and highlighting, the two things most students do, were rated low.6

A simple spaced repetition schedule

You don't need a perfect formula. A common rule of thumb is to review on day 1, day 3, day 7 and day 14, with each gap a little longer than the last. Think of it as a starting point, not a law: the research shows the best gap depends on when you need the material, and no single schedule has been proven best for everyone.

  1. Day 1: learn the material, then close your notes and write down what you remember.
  2. Day 3: quiz yourself before you look. Mark what you missed.
  3. Day 7: quiz again, spending most of your time on the misses.
  4. Day 14 and after: keep going with longer gaps until the test, or for as long as you want to remember it.

If your exam is close, shrink the gaps. If it's months away, stretch them out.

Practical tips

  • Start earlier than feels needed. Spacing needs days to work with. Even two short sessions a few days apart beat one long one.
  • Keep sessions short. Twenty to twenty-five minutes of quizzing is plenty. A timer helps, which is the idea behind the Pomodoro Technique.
  • Use one big flashcard stack. Shuffle it, and don't drop a card the first time you get it right. Bring it back a few more times.
  • Mix topics. Going back and forth between subjects or chapters spaces each one out naturally.
  • Write the plan down. Put review days in your calendar or task list so you don't have to remember to remember.
  • Let apps help, if you like. Flashcard apps can schedule reviews for you. Paper works fine too.

The hardest part is usually starting the first session, not the method. If that's where you get stuck, these small tricks for procrastination can help.

What the research doesn't say

Most spacing studies use word lists, facts, vocabulary or short passages, often in a lab. The effect is strong for that kind of memory. There's less research on things like solving new kinds of problems or writing essays, so expect spacing to help most with what you need to recall. Exact schedules and big percentage claims you see online usually come from apps or blogs, not from studies, so take them lightly.

Common questions

Is spaced repetition better than cramming?

For remembering things longer than a day or two, yes. Cramming can get you through a test tomorrow, but spaced study holds up much better a week or more later.

How long should the gaps be?

It depends on when you need the material. For a test about a week away, a day or two between reviews is a good start. For something you want to keep for months, gaps of a few weeks work well.

Does spaced repetition work for studying for exams?

It works best for facts, terms, formulas and ideas you need to recall. Combine it with practice questions, and start at least a week or two before the exam.

Why does spacing feel less effective than rereading?

Rereading makes material feel familiar, which is easy to mistake for knowing it. Spaced quizzing feels harder because you're actually pulling the memory back up, and that effort is what helps it stick.
