What is body doubling? Why focusing next to someone works

Body doubling means doing a task while someone else is there with you. They don't help with the task. They just keep you company while you both get on with your own work. It sounds almost too simple, and that's the point.

Body doubling, in plain words

A body double is a person who sits with you while you work. They might be a friend at the same table, a roommate on the couch, a coworker on a video call, or a room of strangers in a library. Nobody checks your work or tells you what to do. You simply share the time.

The term comes from ADHD coaching, where people noticed that hard-to-start tasks got easier with someone else in the room. Today all kinds of people use it: students cramming for finals, freelancers working from home, writers, and anyone who struggles to start the boring thing.

Why it works

Research on body doubling itself is still young, so it's best to think of it as a habit that helps many people rather than a proven treatment. But a few well-known ideas explain why it feels so effective.

A little gentle accountability

When someone sits down with you to work, you've made a small, quiet promise. It's easier to open the document when you know you said "I'm going to write my essay" out loud.

People focus better around other people

Psychologists have studied "social facilitation" for over a hundred years: for simple and familiar tasks, many people work faster and steadier when others are around. It's the same reason a busy café or library can feel easier to work in than your bedroom.

Starting stops being so hard

The hardest part of most tasks is the first five minutes. A shared start time ("let's begin at 2") takes the decision away from you. Once you've started, momentum usually carries you.

It's less lonely

Working alone for hours can feel heavy. Having company, even silent company, makes long study sessions feel more like something you do together.

Ways to try body doubling

  • With a friend. Sit at the same table, or start a video call and mute yourselves. Say what you'll work on, then work.
  • In a public place. Libraries, cafés and coworking spaces are full of quiet body doubles.
  • "Study with me" videos. Put on a long video of someone else studying and work alongside it.
  • Focus apps. Apps like Focusland give you a room of others to focus with, any time, without planning a call.

How to make a session work

  1. Pick one task. Small and clear beats big and vague. "Outline chapter 2" beats "work on thesis".
  2. Say it out loud or write it down. Naming the task is half the accountability.
  3. Use a timer. A short block, like 25 minutes, is easy to commit to. This is the Pomodoro Technique, and it pairs perfectly with body doubling.
  4. Take real breaks. Stand up, stretch, get water. Then start the next round.
  5. Keep it low pressure. If you only manage one round, that still counts.

Common questions

Is body doubling only for ADHD?

No. The idea grew popular in the ADHD community, but anyone who finds it hard to start or stay on a task can try it.

Does the other person have to be doing the same thing?

No. Everyone works on their own task. You're sharing time and space, not the work.

Does it work online?

For many people, yes. Video calls, study-with-me videos and focus apps all give you that "someone is here with me" feeling.

How long should a session be?

Start with 25 minutes and a 5 minute break. Go longer once that feels easy.
