How to work from home without getting distracted

By Rosebud Studio, the makers of Focusland. Updated November 9, 2026.

At home, nobody walks past your desk, and the fridge, the laundry and your phone are all a few steps away. If your workday keeps drifting, it isn't a flaw in you. Home was built for living, not working. A few small changes to your space, your phone and your day can make it a lot easier to settle in.

Does working from home make it harder to focus?

Not always. In one of the best known studies, a large Chinese travel company let call center staff who volunteered work from home for nine months, and picked who got to go by lottery. The home group did about 13% better, partly because they took fewer breaks and sick days, and partly because they handled more calls per minute in a quieter space. Fewer of them quit, too. But after the trial, many chose to go back to the office, and loneliness was a common reason.1

That was one company and one kind of job, so it doesn't prove home is better for you. A big review of telecommuting research came to a calmer conclusion: the effects on performance and job satisfaction tend to be small and mostly positive, and they depend a lot on the person, the job and how many days are spent at home.2 The same review pointed to two common struggles: feeling cut off from other people, and work and home life blurring into each other.

So home can be a good place to focus. It just doesn't set itself up that way.

Why distractions cost more than they seem

A quick glance at a message feels harmless. In a study of interrupted work, people who were interrupted actually finished their tasks a bit faster, but they reported more stress, more frustration, more time pressure and more effort.3 In other words, you can keep up with interruptions for a while, but it wears you out. That tired, scattered feeling at 4 PM often comes from a hundred tiny switches, not from the work itself.

8 small ways to stay focused at home

These are practical ideas, some backed by research and some simply common sense. Pick two or three to try this week rather than all eight at once.

1. Give work its own spot

It doesn't need to be an office. A corner of the table, a particular chair or a small desk by the window is enough. When you sit there, you work. When you leave it, you stop. Over time the spot itself starts to tell your brain what time it is. If you can, keep it out of your bedroom and away from the TV.

2. Start the day like you're going somewhere

Without a commute, it's easy to slide from bed straight into email. A short start routine helps: get dressed, make a drink, take a five minute walk around the block, then sit down. It works like a pretend commute, a clear line between home time and work time.

3. Move your phone out of reach

You may have heard that just having your phone nearby drains your brainpower. The evidence is less dramatic than the headlines. A 2024 meta-analysis that pooled many of these studies found only a small effect on working memory and no clear effect on other kinds of thinking, and it noted that many of the studies were weak.4 The simple reason still stands, though. If the phone is in another room, you can't pick it up without thinking. Put it on a shelf, in a drawer or by the front door while you work.

4. Check messages in batches

Constant checking keeps half your mind on your inbox. In one experiment, people who were asked to check email only three times a day for a week felt less stressed than when they could check as often as they liked.5 The stress difference was modest, and it was one week with a small group, but it's an easy thing to try. Pick a few set times, like after each focus block, and close the inbox in between. If your job needs fast replies, try shorter gaps instead of none.

5. Work in timed blocks

An open-ended day invites wandering. A block of 25 or 50 minutes, with a short break after, gives your work a clear shape and makes it easier to ignore the dishes until the timer ends. The Pomodoro technique is a simple way to start, and here's more on how long breaks should be.

6. Write down one task before each block

"Work on the report" is easy to drift away from. "Write the summary paragraph" isn't. Decide on the one thing before the timer starts, so you aren't choosing while also trying to focus.

7. Tell the people you live with

Kids, partners, roommates and pets can't read your mind. A simple signal helps: headphones on means busy, a note on the door with when you'll be free, or a quick "I'm in a block until 11" out loud. Short, clear blocks are easier for others to respect than "I'm working all day."

8. End the day with a short plan

Unfinished work has a way of following you to the couch. In a set of experiments, people who made a specific plan for an unfinished goal had fewer stray thoughts about it afterwards than people who didn't.6 Before you stop, spend two minutes writing down tomorrow's first task and where you left off. Then close the laptop and leave your work spot. For more on why open tasks nag at us, see the Zeigarnik effect.

When it's the loneliness, not the distractions

Sometimes the trouble isn't the fridge or the phone. It's that the house is too quiet and nobody else is working. Plenty of people find it easier to focus with someone else around, even someone doing their own thing. That's the idea behind body doubling. A friend on a video call, a library or a café can all give you that feeling of working side by side.

If you find focusing hard every day, in every setting, and it's getting in the way of your work or life, it's worth talking to a doctor. These tips aren't a treatment for ADHD or any other condition.

Common questions

Why can't I focus when I work from home?

Usually because home is full of cues for everything except work: chores, snacks, your bed, your phone, and no one around to keep you on track. It's a setup problem more than a willpower problem. A set work spot, timed blocks and a phone out of reach help a lot of people.

Is it better to work from home or in an office?

It depends on you and your job. Research finds small, mostly positive effects of working from home on average, but people differ a lot. Some love the quiet. Others miss the company and the routine. Many do best with a mix.

How do I stop doing chores during work hours?

Save them for your breaks. A five minute break is a good time to empty the dishwasher or start a load of laundry, and it gets you moving. Then come back when the break ends.

What should I do when I get distracted anyway?

Notice it, skip the guilt, and go back to the one task you wrote down. If you've drifted far, start a fresh short block. Getting back on track quickly matters more than never drifting.
