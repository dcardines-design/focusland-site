The Zeigarnik effect: why unfinished tasks stick in your head

By Rosebud Studio, the makers of Focusland. Updated October 27, 2026.

You close your laptop, but the half-written essay follows you to dinner, to the shower and into bed. That nagging feeling has a name people often use: the Zeigarnik effect. Here's where the idea comes from, what newer research actually says, and a few gentle ways to quiet the loose ends.

What is the Zeigarnik effect?

In the 1920s, a young psychologist named Bluma Zeigarnik, working in Kurt Lewin's lab in Berlin, gave people a series of small tasks: puzzles, sums, little crafts. Some tasks they were allowed to finish. Others were interrupted halfway. Afterwards she asked them to list the tasks they remembered. In her studies, people tended to recall the interrupted tasks better than the finished ones.1

The idea behind it was simple. Starting a task creates a kind of inner tension, a pull toward finishing. Finishing releases it. Leave a task open, and the tension stays, keeping the task close to the front of your mind.

That story is a big reason the "Zeigarnik effect" shows up in so many productivity tips. But the research since then is more mixed than the tips suggest.

What newer research says

A 2025 meta-analysis by Romain Ghibellini and Beat Meier pulled together decades of studies on interrupted tasks. They found no overall memory advantage for unfinished tasks. People did not reliably remember the interrupted ones better.2

What they did find was a steady tendency to go back to unfinished tasks. This is called the Ovsiankina effect, after Maria Ovsiankina, who studied it in the same lab. When something is left open, people often feel a pull to resume it when they get the chance.2

So the honest version is this: "you'll remember unfinished things better" is shaky. "Unfinished things keep tugging at you" holds up much better. The authors suggest the original memory effect may depend on the situation and the person, such as how involved someone feels in the task.

Why open loops follow you home

That tug isn't only a lab curiosity. In a diary study, Christine Syrek and colleagues followed 59 employees over 12 weeks. On weeks when people ended Friday with more unfinished tasks, they tended to dwell on work over the weekend, and that brooding was linked to worse sleep.3

If you've ever lain awake replaying a to-do list, this will sound familiar. An open task can keep pulling your attention even when you can't do anything about it right now.

The good news: a plan can close the loop

You don't always have to finish a task to quiet it. In a set of experiments, E. J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister found that unfinished goals caused more intruding thoughts and got in the way of other work. But when people made a specific plan for when and how they'd finish the goal, those effects went away, even though the task itself was still not done.4

In other words, your mind seems to relax once it trusts there's a plan. Writing "finish the intro on Thursday at 10, at my desk" can do some of the work that finishing would.

Ways to use this when you study or work

These ideas come from the research above, plus plain common sense. Try one or two, not all at once.

  • Do a two-minute brain dump at the end of the day. Write every open task on paper or in an app, so it's out of your head and somewhere you trust.
  • Give each loose end a next step and a time. "Email Sam about the slides, tomorrow after lunch" is a plan. "Slides" is a worry.
  • Stop at an easy spot to pick back up. If the pull to resume is real, use it. End a session mid-sentence or with a note like "next: add the second example". Starting again feels smaller.
  • Keep one main task per session. Lots of half-started things means lots of tugs at once. Pick one task for the next 25 minutes and let the rest wait on the list.
  • Close the day on purpose. A little end-of-work ritual, like checking off what's done and writing tomorrow's first task, tells your brain the day is finished even if the project isn't.
  • Use the tug to start. If procrastination is the problem, just begin for five minutes. Once a task is open, you may find you want to come back to it.

Short, timed sessions pair well with this. The Pomodoro Technique gives every round a clear finish line, and a quick note before each break makes it easy to pick up where you left off.

When it's more than loose ends

Everyone thinks about unfinished work sometimes. If racing thoughts keep you up most nights, or worry about tasks feels constant and heavy, it's worth talking to a doctor or counselor. These tips are everyday habits, not a treatment.

Common questions

Is the Zeigarnik effect real?

Partly. The original claim, that people remember unfinished tasks better, has been hard to repeat, and a 2025 meta-analysis found no overall memory advantage. The related tendency to go back to unfinished tasks held up well.

How do I stop thinking about unfinished work at night?

Before you stop for the day, write down each open task with a concrete next step and a time to do it. Research suggests a specific plan can quiet the intruding thoughts an unfinished goal causes.

Should I stop studying in the middle of something on purpose?

It can help you start again, since people tend to want to resume open tasks. Leave yourself a short note about the next step. Don't count on it to make you remember more, though; for memory, spaced repetition has much stronger evidence.

What's the difference between the Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effects?

The Zeigarnik effect is about memory: recalling unfinished tasks better. The Ovsiankina effect is about action: feeling pulled to go back and finish them.
